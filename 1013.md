This paper introduces the propensity score, which is the conditional probability of receiving a treatment given the observed pretreatment covariates. The paper shows that the propensity score is a scalar balancing score that the distribution of observed covairates is same between treatment group and control group, conditioning on the propensity score. Therefore, we could adjust for the propensity score rather than a large set of covariates. Assume that the treatment assignment is independent of the potential outcomes given the observed covariates and that the probability is within 0 and 1. Then, the adjustment for the propensity score can give unbiased estimates of average treatment effects. The small and large sample theories are summarized in the paper, and the authors discuss three applications: matched sampling,  multivariate adjustment by subclassification, and visual representation of multivariate covariance adjustment. They compare the coronary artery bypass surgery with the drug therapy, and the observational study shows the results that the subclassification into five groups by propensity score improved the balance across several covariates.

This paper is different from previous papers we have read in this semester, but it is interesting to know the single propensity score can help to adjust for the complicated multivariate problem. If there are many confounding variables, propensity score seems to be helpful. But it seems that the propensity score is limited by the "strongly ignorable treatment assignment" assumption, since propensity score can only adjust for observed covariates. The authors use an example to show that the traditional adjustment method on covariates performs poorly when the treatment and control groups have different covariate structure. Therefore, this indicates that the design and assumptions behind the treatment assignment are important when we use propensity score to estimate.
