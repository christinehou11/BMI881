## 0929

Hernan et. al reanalyzed the data from Nurses' Health Study (NHS) cohort by mimicking the experiment designs and intention to treat (ITT) analysis of the Women's Helath Initiative (WHI) randomized trial. They aim to investigate why earlier observational findings suggested that postmenopausal estrogen plus progestin lowered coronary heart disease (CHD) risk, while WHI trial found increased risk. They set up the NHS data as a series of nonrandomized trials, comparing women who initiate therapy with those who do not. When they align the timing of treatment initiation and follow-up more closely with WHI, the results become more similar: CHD risk appears higher soon after initation, especially among women starting therapy at least 10 years after menopause. The authors argue that differences in time since menopause and length of follow-up explain much of the apparent disagreement. There are a few comments after the main paper, and I think the commenters generally recognize the importance of treatment timing. But they hold different opinions on applying ITT analysis to observational data. Some commenters think the reanalysis as a useful way to compare the studies, but some think that the lack of randomization and assumptions should be ajusted for adherence.

From this collection of paper, I was surprised that changing when follow-up begins could substantially change the estimated early risk. It shows how a design choice can affect which CHD events enter the analysis. I also found that the debate over adherence adjustment useful: the inverse probability weighting addresses a clear problem, but its results depend on adequately measuring and modeling why women stop or start therapy. Finally, this debates indicates that the divergence or discrepancies in study results may come from the analytic and design selections rather than the fundamental failures of observational research.

Questions:
1. Does mimicking the intentional to treat (ITT) framework in observation cohort studies effectively replicate the setting of randomized trial, or does it combine the worst limitaions of both designs by introducing exposure misclassfication without achieving randomization?

2. When might inverse probability weighting give a better estimate of continuous therapy than a conventional time-varying Cox model? How should we interpret the apparent difference by time since menopause when the subgroup estimates are imprecise?
