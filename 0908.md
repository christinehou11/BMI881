The authors discussed the interesting “contradiction” of information display in statistical literature: the statisticians frequently insert visualizations, but they still heavily rely on tables in their published work. The authors included a few examples to redesign several numeric tables as graphs to determine whether the results can be represented accurately. The table formats involve standard errors, percentages, regression estimates, etc. Among these examples, the authors demonstrate various strategies such as separating into small plots, connecting related data with lines, using appropriate axes/directions/locations, and avoid unnecessary labels. They generally argued how the graphs can emphasize the comparisons and trends over time, but they don’t explicitly state that tables are useless that tables are still important if people need to read exact numeric values. Finally, they mention that all tables could be converted into great graphs if they are fully understood and nicely designed.

I found this article very interesting, and it made me recall every time I decided to whether to insert a plot or a table in homework assignments or published work. In my practical work, graphs are always the first choice because they help me to identify the distribution of data points, and some patterns are necessary for determining downstream analytic steps. Compared with plots, tables with thousands of rows are virtually impossible for me to use to obtain meaningful insights. However, I still recognize tables as essential secondary tools. There were many times when the data points on graphs overlapped or were extremely close to each other, and I had to look up exact numeric values in the corresponding table such that I could know the subtle difference and provide firm support for specific analytic statements. For example, when I worked with gene expression data, I usually found PCA plot much more informative for understanding the overall distribution and separation of samples than looking directly at the large matrix of PC scores. Also, I realized that the selection of plot versus table can depend on the topic of project and the research field.
 
### Discussion questions:
1. During the peer review process, many reviewers or editors tend to suggest authors to add more plots or convert some tables into graphs. I wonder whether these editorial comments are more subjective with personal preferences rather than real improvements on data clarity. If the suggestions seem not helpful, how could authors decide to accept or reject the suggestion?
2. Current software packages enable us to generate beautiful and complex graphs with customized color palettes, shapes, and layers. However, are these always necessary? How could we recognize, establish, or balance the boundary between aesthetic design and clear statistical reflection?
