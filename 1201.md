## 1201

Both papers focus on the gene set enrichmet analysis (GSEA) and they demonstrate how it could be helpful to identify the coordinated biological changes that the individual gene analysis may not be able to. Mootha and colleagues explain this approach using the skeletal muscle study, and this study includes individuals with different tolarence on glucose. The results are not significant from individual gene analysis after correcting the multiple testing. However, GSEA identifies the reduced expression of oxidative phosphorylation genes in diabetic muscle. What's more, a coregulated subset responds to PGC-1$\alpha$, suggesting a potential regulatory mechanism. In another paper, Subramanian and colleagues apply GSEA on a more general framework. They use the weighted enrichment score, phenotype-label permutations, and false discovery rate adjustment. The experiments show that the pathway analysis can identify shared biological patterns across studies even though some significant individual genes show little overlap.

Both papers are very helpful and interesting to read.  I am especially impressed that these papers change the unit of analysis to biologically related gene sets to solve the issues happened among individual gene analysis. Better discovery isn't just about choosing the correct error-control procedure, it's also about making the hypothesis on how pathways actually work in biology. Moving from the diabetes study to the full GSEA method makes this point clearly, especially when small changes across many genes add up to a strong signal. Using several known genes can favor familiar biology and miss less studied pathways; overlapping gene sets can make several results seem separate when they really share the same genes. Also, while the PGC-1$\alpha$ tests support the link to gene control, they still don't show whether lower oxidative phosphorylation helps cause diabetes or just happens with it.
