Generally, the authors introduce the new statistical approach that applies well on multiple hypothesis testing. They propose false discovery rate (FDR), which is defined as the expected proportion of false positives among rejected hypotheses. Traditional methods control the familywise error rate (FWER), which is the probability of making at least one false rejection. However, FWER can be too conservative and greatly reduce statistical power, and the situation gets worse if many hypotheses are tested at the same time. The authors talks about a simple step-up procedure that orders p-values and compares them with progressively increasing thresholds to control the FDR under independence assumptions. They mentioned that FDR control is same as FWER control when all null hypotheses are true, but can be more powerful when some hypotheses are false. They use simulations and a clinical trial example to show that their proposal identifies more true discoveries while controlling the error reasonably.

This paper does not reject an old concept totally and further propose a new definition, but providing a different perspective on what helps a meaningful statistical error instead. The authors argue that a few false discoveries could be fine when we aim to identify meaningful associations. This reminds me about current genomic research, where thousands of hypotheses are tested and over-conservative corrections could ignore some important biological signals. In applications such as gene expression analysis, tests are often correlated because genes participate in shared biological pathways or regulatory networks. This raises questions about how reliably the original procedure controls FDR in realistic high-dimensional datasets. I also think the clinical trial example highlights a concern: although FDR control improves power, accepting some false discoveries may have very different consequences in exploratory biological research compared with clinical decision-making. Therefore, choosing between FDR and FWER control should depend not only on statistical efficiency but also on the scientific consequences of incorrect conclusions.

## Questions
1. In which scientific applications would accepting a proportion of false discoveries be reasonable, and when would controlling the probability of even one false positive be more appropriate?
2. Given that many biological measurements are correlated, what additional considerations or modifications might be necessary when applying FDR control?
