Donald Berry evaluates the benefits and risks of screening mammography for women aged 40–49 using results from eight randomized trials. Although the combined trials suggest an 18% reduction in breast-cancer mortality, Berry argues that a standard fixed-effects analysis understates uncertainty because the trials differ in their populations, screening schedules, follow-up periods, and analytical methods. His Bayesian hierarchical analysis accounts for this heterogeneity and estimates an approximately 85% probability that screening would reduce mortality in a new cohort, but its predictive interval also allows for no benefit or possible harm. Berry translates the estimated benefit into more understandable terms: screening may add approximately 1.4 days of life expectancy within 15 years, or about 5.3 days if the mortality benefit continues throughout life. He emphasizes that this modest and uncertain benefit must be balanced against false-positive results, unnecessary biopsies, pain, anxiety, and the detection of cancers that might never become life-threatening.

I found it particularly interesting that a statistically significant pooled result became much less certain after differences among the trials were considered. The paper also shows how presenting only a relative mortality reduction can make a small absolute benefit appear larger than it is. Berry’s use of additional life expectancy makes the effect easier to interpret, although describing an individual woman as the “next trial” may oversimplify differences in personal risk. I was also concerned by the methodological problems he identified, especially the inconsistent definitions of breast-cancer deaths and possible evaluation bias. Overall, the paper makes a persuasive case that screening recommendations should communicate uncertainty and absolute benefits rather than relying only on statistical significance or a single pooled estimate.
