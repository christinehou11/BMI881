Donald Berry evaluated the evidence from 8 randomized controlled trials on the benefits and risks of screening mammography for women aged 40 - 49. Berry argues that the traditional methods with fixed effects have the wrong assumptions of trial homogeneity and definitive reduction in breast cancer mortality. The Bayesian hierarchical model explains the heterogenity, so it reflects how uncertain the screening benfits can be, which the mortality of an individual woman can range from 17% increase to 51% reduction. Berry evalauted the methodolgical issues across several Swedish trials and the Canadian studiy, and he also translates the relative risk metrics into absolute terms. Furthermore, Kopans and Halpern critique Berry for defending the Canadian trial, dismissing statistical power, and subsitituing frequentist principles with Bayesian analysis, while Berry counters that statistial significance does not equate to clinical utility and that patients deserve transparent, uninflated risk-benefit data to make persnalized decisions.

One thing surprised me was the difference between how mammography is commonly viwed and how small its average benefit may be for women in their forties. The 18% reduction in breast cancer mortality sounds substantial, but the absolute benefit is small. The clinical supporters such as Kopans emphasize the possible lives saved by screening, while Berry emphasizes uncertainty, study bias, false positives, and overdiagnosis. This dabate shows that the same evidecnce can lead to different recommendations, depending on how the results are presented and which benefits or risks are given the most importance.
