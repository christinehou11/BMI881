## 1119

The authors propose using q-values to assess statistical significance in genomewide studies, and thousands of hypotheses are tested at the same time. They mentioned that it could be too restrictive if researchers want to control even one false positive if they aim to identify many promising findings. They propose to use the false discovery rate (FDR), computing the expected proportion of false positives among discoveries. A feature’s q-value represents the minimum FDR associated with a significant threshold that includes that feature. The method estimates the proportion of null hypotheses from the observed p-value distribution, and this method allows greater power. When this approach was applied to gene expression data to compare BRCA1- and BRCA2-associated breast tumors, they successfully identify more than 100 significant genes at a q-value threshold of 0.05, while there are only 88 genes identified using Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Tuesday's paper and Thursday's paper are providing differnet methods to improve the power in multiple testing. The authors from last paper reconsider what error we should control, and they think that giving the tolerance to a small proportion of false discoveries can be more useful. Today's paper has the agreement with the last paper, and the authors suggest estimating the proportion of true null hypotheses and using information in the data to make the analysis less conservative. The real-world application is quite interesting because the difference is larger than I had expected. Meanwhile, this improvement raises a question about the balance between power and reliability: how much confidence should we place in additional discoveries when their identification depends on estimating the null proportion? The conservative method is more helpful when the estimation is uncertain, but today's paper also introduces an interesting approach in some genomic studies.
